CIT-0006-25 — Reports Navigation Used

The 30-second version

An 11-page CalSAWS guide describing how county workers find and run reports. It drove four things in the demo’s Reports module: how the screen is organised, the live Timeliness dashboard, the decision to compute every number in real time instead of overnight, and the county and worker filters. It is a user-interface source — it changed how reporting works, never a benefit calculation.

Where to see it: Reports → all six tabs, and the Timeliness tab in particular.

Type: CalSAWS Quick Guide Dated: 11.15.2024 Pages: 11 Verified: downloaded & text-extracted 2026-07-31

calsaws.org/…/CIT-0006-25-Reports-Navigation.pdf

What this document is

CalSAWS publishes it for county staff. It sets no policy and defines no benefit calculation — it explains how a worker finds and runs a report. Treat it as a user-interface source, not a rules source: it shaped how reporting feels. It never touched a dollar figure.

ACIN I-46-25 — CalFresh FFY2026 Cost-of-Living Adjustments Partially used

The 30-second version

An 8-page CDSS notice publishing every CalFresh dollar figure that takes effect 1 October 2025 — maximum allotments, deductions, income standards, and the standard utility allowance. It supplied the FFY2026 numbers the demo’s rules table runs on, and the automated test suite pins one household to $686/month because of them. It is a numbers source: it set the values, not the arithmetic, and its rules on resources, reporting thresholds and recipient noticing were not built.

Where to see it: Admin → Rules — every figure below is a live, editable row. Then run any CalFresh determination: the EDBC trace names each figure as it is applied.

Type: CDSS All County Information Notice Effective: 10.01.2025 (FFY 2026) Pages: 8 Verified: downloaded & text-extracted 2026-07-31

cdss.ca.gov/…/ACINs/2025/I-46_25.pdf

What this document is

CDSS publishes it once a year to tell all 58 counties what the CalFresh numbers become on 1 October. It changes no procedure and no calculation — it republishes the figures the calculation already uses, with Attachment I carrying the full tables. Treat it as a rate source, not a rules source. Its practical value to the build was exactness: every CalFresh dollar amount in the demo has a page and a table behind it.

How it was used — five traceable decisions

01 The maximum allotment table is the notice’s table, value for value

The business point. The maximum allotment is the ceiling on a household’s monthly CalFresh benefit. Every CalFresh dollar the demo produces starts from this row and works down. It is transcribed from page 3, including the $218 add-on for each person beyond eight.

Example you can click. A household of three shows a $785 ceiling in the EDBC trace. The demo shipped on FFY2025 figures and was moved to these: max allotment $768 → $785, standard deduction $204 → $209, shelter cap $712 → $744, SUA added at $663. That delta is stated on the public Reference tab.

04 Shelter cap, standard utility allowance, and homeless deduction

The business point. Housing is the single largest deduction in a CalFresh budget, and all three of its published figures come from this notice: the $744 cap on the excess shelter deduction, the $663 standard utility allowance, and the standard deduction for households with no shelter cost.

Example you can click. Enter rent of $1,400 on a household of three and the trace shows the excess shelter deduction hitting the $744 ceiling.

One deliberate rounding

Attachment I states the homeless shelter deduction to the cent — $198.99, up from $190.30. The demo stores it as a whole dollar, $199. Every other figure on this page is exact to the source.

05 The $24 minimum allotment

The business point. A one- or two-person household that qualifies but calculates to a tiny benefit is paid a floor amount instead of nothing. The notice raised that floor from $23 to $24, and $24 is what the demo pays.

Example you can click. Push a single applicant’s income to the top of the eligible range — the result lands on $24/month rather than $0.

The whole chain, in one number

The end-to-end test suite asserts a fixed benefit for a golden household — $686/mo — and that number is only reachable with this notice’s figures. Household of three, $1,600 earned, $1,400 rent:

1,600 − 320 (20% earned) − 209 (standard deduction)      = 1,071
1,400 rent − 535.50 (half of 1,071) = 864.50 → capped at    744   ← $744 shelter cap
1,071 − 744                                              =   327   net income
785 (max allotment) − 99 (30% of 327, rounded up)        =   686

Where this document did not reach

Stated plainly so the trace is complete. Substantial parts of the notice are not in the software:

  • Thrifty Food Plan re-evaluation (page 3). The notice’s rule for very large households — an additional 22 percent per member for households of 9 or more, capped at 18 — is not built. The demo extrapolates at a flat $218 per additional person with no cap.
  • The limited and telephone utility allowances — $170 and $20 (page 4). Only the full SUA exists; there is no tiered utility allowance.
  • Resource limits — $3,000, and $4,500 for elderly or disabled households, which also serve as the substantial-winnings threshold (page 4). The CalFresh calculation runs no resource test.
  • The Income Reporting Threshold at 130% of poverty and the 10-day mid-period reporting duty (pages 5 and 8). Neither the value nor the reporting obligation exists in the demo.
  • The 130% gross standard and the 165% elderly/disabled separate-household standard (page 7). The demo’s only gross test is California’s 200% broad-based categorical eligibility test.
  • The CF 11 recipient notice — the obligation to notify every CalFresh household on or before 1 October 2025. The demo’s correspondence module has no COLA mass notice.
  • The 90-day state hearing window, bracketed issuance tables, and translation requirements.

Nothing in the notice was contradicted. These parts were out of scope for the demo.

CIT-0355-22 — CalSAWS Configuration Guide Partially used

The 30-second version

A 146-page reference for county administrators, covering how a county sets up its staff, its task rules, its approval levels, and its security roles inside CalSAWS. Two things in the demo come from it: every staff account carries a real CalSAWS-shaped Worker ID, and the public guide quotes the platform’s exact security scale — 29 roles, 1,498 security groups. It is an administration source, and the demo took the surface of it: the org hierarchy the Worker ID encodes was never built as data, and none of the county-configurable settings the guide exists to explain are in the software.

Where to see it: Admin → Users (the Worker ID column), Reports → Productivity, and Guide → Reference → the CalSAWS cornerstone panel.

Where this document did not reach

This is a 146-page configuration manual and the demo built two things from it. Everything below is described in the guide and is absent from the software:

  • The org hierarchy as data. There is no Office, Section, Unit, Position or Bank table anywhere in the schema — a staff profile carries role, county, worker ID and title, nothing more. The hierarchy the Worker ID encodes survives only as characters inside that string.
  • County-configurable authorization levels. The guide’s whole point on page 86 is that a county chooses No / 1st Level / 2nd Level per action. The demo has one hardcoded supervisor step and no configuration surface, so the Section-based 2nd-level lookup on page 20 has nothing to run against.
  • Benefit Issuance Thresholds. No dollar threshold exists anywhere; the rules table has no issuance-threshold field, so nothing blocks a worker on payment size.
  • The entire task-configuration chapter — Task Types, Sub-Types, Categories, Task Banks, the Automated Action inventory, Get Next and Get Next Limit, Bundle Case Tasks, Document Routing Rules, Task Reassignment.
  • Global / Local / Task navigation. Named on the guide’s Reference page as a description of CalSAWS; not implemented as navigation.
  • The rest of Chapter Two — Schedule, Correspondence configuration, IEVS Batch Assignment, Valuables, Mileage Rate, EBT Printers, Workload Assignment, MAGI Referral Assignments, Non-County Staff, Flags, Error Prone / High Risk — and Chapter Three’s role-creation tooling (Conflicting Security Roles, the Copy feature, security-group assignment). Nothing in the guide was contradicted. It was read for shape, and two pieces of that shape were carried over.

CIT-0074-23 — Wave 3 Yellow Banner Case Review Partially used

The 30-second version

A 5-page CalSAWS transmittal telling counties what to do when a case comes out of the CalWIN-to-CalSAWS conversion with a benefit mismatch: CalSAWS marks it with a yellow banner and refuses to save the result until a worker reviews it. The demo took the banner itself — its exact on-screen wording, and its blocking behaviour — and made yellow_banner a first-class case status. The honest boundary: the demo points that banner at a different trigger (an unresolved income data match, not a conversion mismatch), and the document’s ten-reason mismatch taxonomy and priority-clearing workflow were not built.

Where to see it: open a flagged case from the worker queue — the yellow banner sits at the top of the Case Summary, and the EDBC tab will not produce a saved result until it is resolved.

Type: CalSAWS County Information Transmittal — conversion go-live guidance Distributed: 03.02.2023 Pages: 5 Format: scanned images, no text layer — quotes read from rendered pages Verified: downloaded & page-rendered 2026-07-31

calsaws.org/…/CIT-0074-23-Wave-3-Yellow-Banner-Case-Review-Guide-and-Scenarios-Revised_Redacted.pdf

What this document is

Go-live instructions for the third wave of counties migrating off CalWIN. At cutover CalSAWS re-ran every active case and compared its own answer against the last answer CalWIN gave. Matches saved silently; disagreements got a yellow banner and a manual review. Treat it as a process source — it defines a specific quality gate: when the system is not confident, it stops and puts a human in front of the case.

What this document is

CDSS raises the CalWORKs need standards once a year against the California Needs Index. 7 CFR 273.9 — Income and deductions (SNAP) Partially used

The 30-second version

The federal regulation that defines how a SNAP household’s income is counted and which deductions come off it — the source law behind every CalFresh budget in the country. It supplied the arithmetic of the demo’s CalFresh calculation: the 20 percent earned-income deduction, the $35 medical threshold for elderly and disabled households, the waiver of the gross-income test for those households, the half-of-remaining-income shelter formula and its cap, and the net income limit at 100 percent of poverty. It supplied none of the dollar figures — those come from the annual state COLA notice — and roughly half the section, the long list of income exclusions, is not built at all.

Where to see it: run any CalFresh determination and open the EDBC trace. Steps G1 through N1 are this regulation, in order, each showing its own arithmetic.

Type: Federal regulation — 7 CFR Part 273, SNAP certification Source: eCFR, current edition Pages: n/a — web regulation, no single PDF Verified: fetched & text-extracted 2026-07-31

ecfr.gov/current/title-7/…/part-273/section-273.9

What this document is

Federal law, not state guidance. It fixes what counts as income, what may be excluded, and the five deductions that turn gross income into net income — in the order they must be taken. States set the dollar amounts each October; this section sets the method. Treat it as the rules source for CalFresh: the shape of the calculation is here, and the annual notice fills in the values.

What the document actually says

Verbatim, by paragraph:

“Households which contain an elderly or disabled member shall meet the net income eligibility standards for SNAP. Households which do not contain an elderly or disabled member shall meet both the net income eligibility standards and the gross income eligibility standards for SNAP.”§ 273.9(a)
“The net income eligibility standards for SNAP shall be as follows: … The income eligibility standards for the 48 contiguous States and the District of Columbia, Guam and the Virgin Islands shall be the Federal income poverty levels for the 48 contiguous States and the District of Columbia.”§ 273.9(a)(2) and (a)(2)(i)
“Earned income deduction. Twenty percent of gross earned income as defined in paragraph (b)(1) of this section.”§ 273.9(d)(2)
“Excess medical deduction. That portion of medical expenses in excess of $35 per month, excluding special diets, incurred by any household member who is elderly or disabled as defined in § 271.2.”§ 273.9(d)(3)
“Excess shelter deduction. Monthly shelter expenses in excess of 50 percent of the household’s income after all other deductions in paragraphs (d)(1) through (d)(5) of this section have been allowed. If the household does not contain an elderly or disabled member, as defined in § 271.2 of this chapter, the shelter deduction cannot exceed the maximum shelter deduction limit established for the area.”§ 273.9(d)(6)(ii)

How it was used — five traceable decisions

01 The 20 percent earned income deduction

The business point. Working households have a fifth of their earnings set aside before any test — the regulation’s recognition that wages carry costs. It is the first deduction taken, and it is a rate, not a dollar amount, so it comes from here rather than from any state notice.

Example you can click. EDBC trace, step D1: “Earned income deduction (20%) — $1,600 earned × 20% = −$320.”

02 Elderly and disabled households skip the gross income test

The business point. Two tests exist, and § 273.9(a) says who takes which. A household with a member aged 60 or over, or a disabled member, is judged on net income only. Everyone else must clear both. That branch is the regulation’s, and the demo takes it explicitly — including a trace line saying the test was waived and why, rather than silently skipping it.

Example you can click. Add a 67-year-old to a household and re-run. Step G1 changes from a pass/fail to “Waived — elderly (60+)/disabled household; net test only.”

04 The excess shelter deduction — half of remaining income, capped only for households with no elderly or disabled member

The business point. The most intricate rule in the section, and the one that most often decides a CalFresh amount. Shelter counts only to the extent it exceeds half of what is left after every other deduction, and the cap is lifted entirely for elderly and disabled households. Both halves of that rule are in four lines of code.

Example you can click. Household of three, $1,400 rent: trace step D5 shows the half-income subtraction and then the cap biting. Add an elderly member and the cap comes off — the deduction goes through uncapped and the benefit rises.

The code

platform/src/lib/engine.ts:140–145

const shelter = T.rent + (T.utilities > 0 ? C.sua : 0);
const half    = r2(Math.max(0, net) / 2);
let excess    = r2(Math.max(0, shelter - half));
const capped  = !ed && excess > C.shelterCap;    // cap applies only without an elderly/disabled member
if (capped) excess = C.shelterCap;

The cap’s value ($744) and the utility allowance ($663) are the state’s annual figures, not this section’s. The formula and the exemption are this section’s.

05 The net income limit is 100 percent of the Federal Poverty Level

The business point. After every deduction, the household must land at or under the poverty line for its size. § 273.9(a)(2) sets that standard at the federal poverty levels themselves — the one place in the calculation where the threshold is 100 percent and nothing else.

Example you can click. EDBC trace, step N1: “Net income test (≤ 100% FPL) — Net $327 vs limit $2,221.” Both numbers are derived from one stored table.

Where this document did not reach

A large majority of § 273.9 is not built, and two built pieces depart from it. Stated plainly:

  • The whole of paragraph (c) — income exclusions. Roughly half the section, around twenty enumerated exclusions: educational assistance, reimbursements, vendor payments, non-recurring lump sums, child support received, employment-and-training payments, foster care payments, trust withdrawals, student earnings. The demo sums every income row without filtering.
  • The income taxonomy in paragraph (b) beyond a simple earned/unearned split, including sponsored-alien deeming and pro-rata attribution for disqualified members.
  • The optional child support deduction, § 273.9(d)(5) — even though child support exists as an expense type in the demo’s data model, it is never deducted.
  • The utility allowance machinery in § 273.9(d)(6)(iii) — the individual, limited and telephone standards, HEAP-based entitlement, proration, and mandatory-versus-optional use. The demo has one standard allowance and applies it whenever any utility cost is present.
  • The shelter sub-categories in (d)(6)(ii)(A)–(E) — property taxes, a vacated home, disaster repair — the allowable-medical-cost list in (d)(3)(i)–(x), and the minimum standard deduction levels in (d)(1)(iii).
  • Regional variants for Alaska, Hawaii, Guam and the Virgin Islands, and the annual 1 October reindexing process in (a)(3) and (d)(1)(iii).

Two deliberate departures, so nothing here is read as conformance:

  • The gross income standard is 200 percent of poverty, per California’s broad-based categorical eligibility, where § 273.9(a)(1)(i) sets 130 percent.
  • The homeless shelter deduction uses California’s $199 and applies only when the household has no shelter cost at all; § 273.9(d)(6)(i) sets $143 and directs it at homeless households not receiving free shelter throughout the month.
